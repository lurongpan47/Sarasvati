**Branch**: Silk Road · 中亚·丝路 — first sample. The Dutreuil de Rhins manuscript is the anchor text for this branch: found in the Tarim basin, written in Kharoṣṭhī (a Silk-Road script), in the middle-Indic language (Gāndhārī) that carried early Buddhism from Greater Gandhāra into Central Asia and on to Han China. Even a rough parallel reading illustrates why this branch matters — the same verses that anchor the Pāli tradition were traveling east in a completely different script and dialect at the same time.

## Verse 1 · Dhp 1 · Mind precedes all things (evil)

### Gāndhārī (reconstruction from Pāli parallel ⟨བརྟག⟩)
> Manopuvagama dhama, manoseṭha manomaya;
> manasa ye paduṭheṇa bhaṣadi va karodi va,
> tado ṇa dukha aṇvedi cakra va vahado padaṃ.

*(Reconstruction. Actual Khotan folio reading to be inserted from Brough 1962 or a public-domain transcription. Kharoṣṭhī orthography is highly variable; expect divergence.)*

### English (Sarasvatī draft from Pāli)
> Mind precedes all mental states. Mind is their chief; they are all mind-wrought. If with an impure mind a person speaks or acts, suffering follows him as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws the cart.

### Chinese (Sarasvatī draft, cf. T210 心多為法本)
> 心为诸法先，心为诸法主，诸法唯心造。
> 若以染污心，或语或行时，苦即随其身，如轮随牛足。

### Pāli parallel (Dhp 1, PTS)
> Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā, manoseṭṭhā manomayā;
> manasā ce paduṭṭhena bhāsati vā karoti vā,
> tato naṃ dukkham anveti cakkaṃ va vahato padaṃ.

## Verse 2 · Dhp 2 · Mind precedes all things (good)

### Gāndhārī (reconstruction from Pāli parallel ⟨བརྟག⟩)
> Manopuvagama dhama, manoseṭha manomaya;
> manasa ye pasaṇeṇa bhaṣadi va karodi va,
> tado ṇa suha aṇvedi chaya va aṇavayiṇi.

*(Reconstruction. Verify against actual folio ⟨བརྟག⟩.)*

### English (Sarasvatī draft from Pāli)
> Mind precedes all mental states. Mind is their chief; they are all mind-wrought. If with a pure mind a person speaks or acts, happiness follows him like a shadow that never leaves.

### Chinese (Sarasvatī draft, cf. T210)
> 心为诸法先，心为诸法主，诸法唯心造。
> 若以清净心，或语或行时，乐即随其身，如影恒相随。

### Pāli parallel (Dhp 2, PTS)
> Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā, manoseṭṭhā manomayā;
> manasā ce pasannena bhāsati vā karoti vā,
> tato naṃ sukham anveti chāyā va anapāyinī.

## Verse 3 · Dhp 3 · Hatred is not stilled by hatred (statement)

### Gāndhārī (reconstruction from Pāli parallel ⟨བརྟག⟩)
> Agrocchi maṃ avadhi maṃ, ajiṇi maṃ ahaṣi me,
> ye taṃ upaṇayhaṃdi, veru teṣu ṇa śamadi.

*(Reconstruction. The Khotan manuscript reorders and omits verses; verse-numbering here follows the Pāli, not the Kharoṣṭhī folio. ⟨བརྟག⟩)*

### English (Sarasvatī draft from Pāli)
> "He abused me, he struck me, he defeated me, he robbed me" — in those who harbour such thoughts, hatred is not stilled.

### Chinese (Sarasvatī draft, cf. T210 人若罵我)
> "彼骂我、彼打我、彼胜我、彼夺我"——
> 于怀此念者，怨怒不能息。

### Pāli parallel (Dhp 3, PTS)
> Akkocchi maṃ avadhi maṃ, ajini maṃ ahāsi me,
> ye taṃ upanayhanti, veraṃ tesaṃ na sammati.

## Verse 4 · Dhp 4 · Hatred is stilled only by non-hatred

### Gāndhārī (reconstruction from Pāli parallel ⟨བརྟག⟩)
> Agrocchi maṃ avadhi maṃ, ajiṇi maṃ ahaṣi me,
> ye taṃ ṇa upaṇayhaṃdi, veru teṣu uvaśamadi.

*(Reconstruction. ⟨བརྟག⟩)*

### English (Sarasvatī draft from Pāli)
> "He abused me, he struck me, he defeated me, he robbed me" — in those who do not harbour such thoughts, hatred is stilled.

### Chinese (Sarasvatī draft, cf. T210)
> "彼骂我、彼打我、彼胜我、彼夺我"——
> 于不怀此念者，怨怒自平息。

### Pāli parallel (Dhp 4, PTS)
> Akkocchi maṃ avadhi maṃ, ajini maṃ ahāsi me,
> ye taṃ na upanayhanti, veraṃ tesūpasammati.

**Historical note.** The Dutreuil de Rhins manuscript was recovered in 1892 near Khotan (modern Xinjiang) by members of the Dutreuil de Rhins expedition, split between Paris (Bibliothèque nationale) and St Petersburg, and first partly published by Émile Senart. The full modern edition is John Brough, *The Gāndhārī Dharmapada* (London 1962). It is one of the earliest surviving Buddhist manuscripts in any language, and the language it preserves — Gāndhārī, a north-western Middle Indic — was the working language of the monks who first carried Buddhism into Central Asia and China. The Chinese *Faju jing* (T210, 3rd c. CE) preserves a parallel collection that in places matches the Gāndhārī more closely than the Pāli. This branch of the archive exists to hold texts like this one — texts that survived in the desert, on birch bark, in scripts nobody now reads without training.
